# Patterns that work

Use these as starting points, not templates to copy verbatim. Every example needs a real fact from the recipient's world.

## Openers

### Observation about their work

> Your post about [specific topic] matched what we see in [context].

> Saw that [company] moved [product/team] to [change]. That usually puts pressure on [adjacent process].

> Your [talk/article/launch] mentioned [specific detail]. We ran into the same thing with [context].

### Question about their process

> When [specific situation happens], who notices first: your team, or the customer?

> How much of [role]'s week goes into [manual task]?

> Is [process] still handled in [tool/spreadsheet]?

### Relevant event or change

> Congrats on [real event]. Usually that means [process] gets harder before it gets easier.

> [Company] just opened [role/location/product]. That tends to expose [problem].

### Shared context

> We both sat through [event/talk]. The part about [detail] stuck with me.

> You wrote about [topic]; I've been collecting examples of [related thing] for a while.

### Placeholder when the fact is missing

> [PERSONALIZE: one specific thing you noticed about them, and why it made you write]

Never replace a placeholder with a generic compliment. "Loved your recent post" without a post is worse than a bracket.

## Subject lines

Short, neutral, specific to the recipient's world.

- `week one`
- `setup drop-off`
- `stuck accounts`
- `before the renewal call`
- `handoff notes`
- `onboarding data`
- `a question about [topic]`
- `[their company] + setup`
- `one thing about [process]`

Avoid:

- `Quick question` (overused, vague)
- `Following up`
- `Introduction`
- `Partnership opportunity`
- `Revolutionize your [thing]`
- Anything with `!`, all caps, or emoji

## CTAs

The ask should cost one sentence to answer.

**Interest check**

- "Is this something you're working on?"
- "Does that match what you're seeing?"
- "Worth a look, or not a priority?"

**Permission to say no**

- "If this is already handled, a one-word no is fine."
- "Not a fit? Say so and I'll close the loop."
- "If the timing is wrong, tell me and I'll stop."

**Low-cost next step**

- "Should I send the one-page version?"
- "Want the checklist we use?"
- "Happy to share the data if it's useful."

**High-bar asks to avoid in the first email**

- "Can I get 30 minutes?"
- "When can we jump on a call?"
- "Let's schedule a demo."
- "I'd love to pick your brain."

## Follow-up sequence

Three touches, then stop. Each touch adds something new.

### Touch 1: the original email

One observation, one idea, one question.

### Touch 2: a new angle (3–5 days later)

> One more angle on [problem]: [specific example or number from a real source].
>
> If [opposite situation] is true for you, ignore this.

No guilt. No "just following up". No summary of the first email.

### Touch 3: a clean exit (5–7 days later)

> Last note from me. If [problem] becomes a priority, reply and I'll pick it up.
>
> If not, no hard feelings.

This is not a trick to get a reply. It is a real exit. Mean it.

## Personalization without facts

If the user cannot provide a fact:

1. Ask for one specific thing: a post, a product page, a talk, a job posting, a public number.
2. If none is available, use `[PERSONALIZE: ...]`.
3. Never invent a post, a quote, a metric, or a mutual connection.

## Language

Write in the recipient's language when it is known. Otherwise use the language of the request.

Keep the tone conversational in every language. Direct translations of English idioms often sound wrong; rewrite the idea, not the sentence.

## Format

- Plain text.
- Short paragraphs.
- No bullet lists in the first email.
- At most one link, and only when it is genuinely useful.
- Signature: name and role on one line.

## A note on "personalization"

Personalization is not `{{first_name}}`. It is evidence that you looked at the recipient's world and found something relevant. One specific sentence beats five merge fields.
