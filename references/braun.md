# Josh Braun: sell without rejection

Josh Braun's approach starts from a simple observation: people do not dislike being sold to as much as they dislike feeling cornered. Pressure creates resistance. Curiosity creates replies.

The rules below are a practical reading of that approach, written for this skill. They are not quotes, and this project is not affiliated with Josh Braun.

## Eight rules

### 1. Start with the problem, not the product

The recipient does not wake up wondering about your product. They wake up with a problem they have learned to live with.

Bad:

> We built an AI-powered onboarding platform that helps teams reduce time-to-value.

Better:

> Most new accounts go quiet in week one, and nobody notices until the renewal call.

The first version is about the sender. The second is about a Tuesday the recipient recognizes.

### 2. Poke the bear

A good question makes a cost visible without insulting the recipient. It does not create a problem; it names one that already exists.

Examples:

- "When a new account stalls, does anyone find out before the renewal call?"
- "How much of the CS team's week goes into chasing setup steps?"
- "Is the handoff from sales to onboarding still a spreadsheet?"

The question should be easy to answer from memory. If it requires research, it is not a good opening question.

### 3. Do not pitch

A pitch assumes the recipient is already interested. Most are not. Pitching early answers a question they have not asked.

Replace the pitch with relevance:

- Instead of "Our platform does X, Y, and Z", write "We help [type of team] with [specific problem]."
- Instead of a feature list, give one sentence of context after the problem.
- Instead of social proof, use proof only if it is real, specific, and short.

One sentence about what you do is enough. The email is not the place to close.

### 4. Lower the bar for answering

Every ask has a cost. "Can I get 30 minutes?" costs a calendar decision. "Is this relevant?" costs one word.

Low-bar asks:

- "Is this something you're working on?"
- "Worth a look, or not a priority?"
- "Does that match what you're seeing?"
- "Should I send the one-page version?"

High-bar asks:

- "Do you have 30 minutes this week?"
- "Can we jump on a call?"
- "When can I show you a demo?"

The low-bar question is not timid. It is designed to start a conversation that can grow.

### 5. Give permission to say no

Permission to decline removes the trap. Paradoxically, it increases honest replies.

Examples:

- "If this is already handled, a one-word no is fine."
- "If the timing is wrong, tell me and I'll close the loop."
- "Not a fit? Say so and I'll stop."

Do not overdo it. One line, once. Repeated disclaimers read as insecurity.

### 6. No manufactured urgency

Fake deadlines, fake scarcity, and "last chance" language are easy to detect and expensive to recover from. If there is a real reason to act now, state it plainly. If there is not, do not invent one.

### 7. Talk like a colleague

Write as if you are across the table from the recipient, not presenting to a committee.

- Use contractions.
- Use short sentences.
- Allow one small imperfection.
- Skip "I hope this email finds you well", "I trust you are well", and "Please allow me to introduce myself".

### 8. Detach from the outcome

A cold email is a test, not a plea. Some people will not reply. That is normal. Detachment shows up in the writing: no chasing, no guilt, no "I'll keep this at the top of your inbox".

## What it looks like in practice

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| "I wanted to reach out because..." | "Saw your post about..." |
| "We are a leading provider of..." | "We help [role] with [problem]." |
| "Our platform empowers teams to..." | "Teams use it to [specific outcome]." |
| "Can I get 15 minutes?" | "Is this relevant?" |
| "I know you're busy, but..." | "One question:" |
| "Following up on my last email" | A new angle, in one sentence |
| "Let me know if you'd be interested" | "Worth a look?" |

## Common misreads

- **Poking the bear is not negging.** Do not insult the recipient's process. Name a real cost without blame.
- **Every email does not need a question.** But every cold email needs one clear next step. Usually that is a question.
- **Permission to say no is not self-deprecation.** It is a clean exit, not an apology.
- **Short is not rude.** Brevity signals respect for the recipient's time.
- **No pitch does not mean no context.** Say what you do. Just do not make it the point of the email.
